\begin{center}
    {\textbf{Abstract}}\\[0.20in]
    {\textbf{The Pilot-Proxy Method}}\\[0.18in]
    {Dylan Gormley}\\[0.32in]
\end{center}

North American broadcast television occupies 470--608~MHz, a 138-MHz interval
that maps to $1.34 \le z \le 2.02$ for the redshifted 21\,cm line and therefore
covers roughly one third of CHIME's cosmological observing band.  The received
8-VSB payload can lie below thermal noise in an individual detector frame yet
remain correlated long enough to bias a multi-year intensity-mapping survey.
The problem is therefore not whether television can be detected, but whether a
frame-level mask can remove enough contamination, at an acceptable exposure
cost, for the baryon acoustic oscillation (BAO) measurement to remain practical
in that band.

This dissertation develops the pilot-proxy method around the one feature an
ATSC~A/53 transmitter is required to emit continuously: the suppressed-carrier
pilot.  A matched-filter projection onto the standardized pilot frequency,
normalized by two displaced reference projections, yields a per-frame statistic
that estimates the shelf-to-noise ratio of the whole 6-MHz allocation from a
single monitored coarse channel; a rank-based fine-axis decision turns that
estimate into a mask.  The detector is parameterized for the upgraded CHIME
correlator geometry (2048 inputs, 16384-sample frames, 128-tap weights) and
implemented as an exact fixed-point pipeline whose projections, frozen
fixed-point transform, power sums, and rational decision arithmetic are
bit-reproducible against independent references.

The method is validated on an evidence ladder with one arithmetic contract,
two receiver profiles, and three evidence platforms.  Synthetic proof of
concept uses GNU~Radio and a LimeSDR radio path to measure the pilot
statistic's transfer against known data-shelf SNR and to bound the cost of the
fixed-point implementation against floating point on paired trials.  Offline validation applies the detector to
CHIME's archive of triggered full-array baseband captures on all 23
allocations: it identifies each channel's latest stable transmitter era,
characterizes that era's null distribution, selects a per-channel operating
threshold against a Fisher-forecast tolerance, and reports the spectrum the
mask removes.  Online validation deploys the same arithmetic as a
\texttt{kotekan} stage on the CHORD Pathfinder, whose real-time pipeline is
shared with CHIME's, in an hours-long shadow run.

A Fisher-forecast framework (RFIsher) converts masked fraction and surviving
contamination residual into integration-time cost and parameter-bias
tolerance, and prices each policy against keeping everything, excising the
channel, and the incumbent flaggers.
\stub{v5 archive rerun and Pathfinder shadow run}{Two or three sentences with
the headline results: how many of the 23 channels admit a tolerance-selected
operating point, how many are excision candidates, the band-level
integration-time cost of the selected masks, and the outcome of the
Pathfinder shadow run (hours run, frames dropped, exact-replay agreement).}
The principal result is both an instrument and an auditable decision
framework: exact where the digital contract permits exactness, measured where
the archive supplies evidence, and explicitly bounded where it does not.

from a narrow instrumentation question into a dissertation, and who insisted
throughout that a result is only as good as the record that supports it.  Much

This work rests entirely on instruments and archives built by others.  I thank

measurements depend on.  I thank the Canadian Advanced Network for Astronomical
Research for the storage and compute that hosted the archive survey.  I am grateful to
the members of the WVU Radio Astronomy Instrumentation Group for years of
conversation, code review, and shared debugging.
